Lead story · Venezuela

The Venezuela trade is now real in the numbers — and that's exactly when the politics get loud

For weeks we've tracked the licenses widening. Now it's showing up where it counts — the data. U.S. imports of Venezuelan crude reached roughly 701,000 barrels per day in July, a nine-year high and up about 11% on June. A corridor that was throttled for years is moving real volume again, and Venezuela's ports are busy in a way they haven't been since before the sanctions era.

But here's the turn: the bigger a reopening gets, the more political it becomes. This week, four U.S. Senate Democrats formally challenged the legality of the Pentagon's planned 35% stake in the NABEP oil venture, and regional players — Colombia's Ecopetrol among them — are openly scouting deals in Caracas. Translation for shippers: the volume is here, but the framework underneath it is now being fought over in Washington. That's not a reason to sit out — it's a reason to move carefully and keep your compliance airtight, because a contested policy is one that can shift.

Read the fine print — this is the part that hasn't changed. These remain sector-specific authorizations with conditions, not a blanket lifting of sanctions. Broad Venezuela sanctions stay in force, general (non-oil) cargo still requires full OFAC screening and carrier pre-approval, and — as this week's politics underline — the licenses can be narrowed as fast as they were widened. If you're weighing Venezuela business, get current, qualified legal counsel on exactly what the licenses cover for your cargo and counterparties. Stella Line runs an independent OFAC compliance review on every Venezuela booking and does not itself provide legal or sanctions advice.

1 Global Ocean Freight Snapshot

Away from the Venezuela headline, the story is the peak that simply refuses to break. We've called it "on borrowed time" for two editions — yet transpacific pushed higher again into the end of September, with both U.S. coasts back at peak-season highs. The driver has fully shifted from demand to disruption: weather and congestion, not orders, are what's holding the line. Current levels (source: Freightos Baltic Index, late September 2026):

Macro drivers — weather is doing the carriers' work for them. Typhoon Saudel shut Shanghai and Ningbo for several days and rippled as far north as Busan; with storms hitting since mid-July, the backlog never cleared, and at one point roughly 90 ships were waiting more than a week for Shanghai berths. On top of that, carriers have now switched on their Golden Week (Oct 1) blank sailings, pulling capacity out just as the congestion bites. A second watch-item lands right on our doorstep: Panama Canal low-water restrictions are back in play, raising the odds of transit surcharges that feed straight into East Coast and Caribbean-routed costs. Asia–Europe, by contrast, keeps easing as Suez capacity returns. Underlying demand is softening (the NRF still projects U.S. imports down into October–November), but you won't feel that relief while the ports stay clogged.

Why this matters for the Caribbean

We're now inside the squeeze, not ahead of it. With Golden Week blanks live and Far East congestion unresolved, there is less spare mainline tonnage feeding the Gulf and Caribbean transshipment hubs — so expect tight feeder space and real rollover risk through the first half of October. Watch Panama too: any low-water surcharge is an added line on canal-routed cargo. The playbook is unchanged but more urgent: secure space now, confirm your box is physically loaded rather than rolled, and build a buffer into every delivery promise. Rates should hold firm near-term and only soften once the congestion clears and the transpacific peak finally gives way in Q4 — get your BAF/EFS and any canal surcharge itemized before you commit.

U.S. Sanctions & Compliance — Venezuela

Commercial shipping from the U.S. to Venezuela is subject to active U.S. Treasury OFAC sanctions. While cargo is not categorically prohibited, it requires strict compliance: (1) full SDN screening of all parties; (2) no shipments to government-owned or controlled entities without a specific OFAC license; (3) restricted USD payment channels; and (4) complete booking details at time of request — no blind bookings. All Venezuela cargo is subject to independent OFAC compliance review and carrier pre-approval before any booking is confirmed. Stella Line strongly recommends that customers obtain independent legal and sanctions-compliance counsel before proceeding, and does not itself provide legal or sanctions-compliance advice.

5 Outlook & Commercial Recommendations

We're in the Golden Week squeeze — act, don't wait. Blank sailings are live and Far East congestion is unresolved, so feeder space is genuinely tight right now. Book immediately for October moves, and get written confirmation your box is physically loaded — a cheap rate on a rolled container helps no one.

Price in a Panama Canal surcharge. Low-water restrictions are back on the table. If your cargo routes via the canal, ask now whether a transit surcharge applies and get it quoted as a separate line, so it doesn't land as a surprise after you've committed.

Keep commitments short — relief is coming, just not yet. Underlying demand is softening into Q4, but congestion is masking it. For recurring Caribbean cargo, keep contract windows short (30–60 days) so you can capture rate relief once the ports clear, rather than locking in today's congestion-inflated peak.

Venezuela — the volume is real; your compliance has to be too. Trade is moving and early, well-prepared shippers have the advantage — but the policy is now politically contested, which means it can move. Keep OFAC screening and carrier pre-approval complete before any booking (allow 3–5 business days), and don't commit to customers before confirming carrier acceptance.

Project & break-bulk — talk to us early. The Venezuela oil opening will pull equipment, spares and out-of-gauge cargo behind it. For heavy-lift or project moves, engage us at the planning stage so we can line up the right vessel and stowage before space tightens further.
